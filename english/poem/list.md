## 1 "I Died for Beauty" By Emily Dickinson


I died for Beauty – but was scarce
Adjusted in the Tomb
When One who died for Truth, was lain
In an adjoining Room –

He questioned softly “Why I failed”?
“For Beauty”, I replied –
“And I – for Truth – Themself are One –
We Brethren, are”, He said –

And so, as Kinsmen, met a Night –
We talked between the Rooms –
Until the Moss had reached our lips –
And covered up – our names –


Theme: The poem depicts the profound philosophical unity between truth and beauty. It characterizes them as identical and inseparable concepts that define great human excellence. Additionally, the poem highlights the absolute power of mortality. It shows us/reveals that time and death eventually erase all human identity, noble achievements and names entirely.

## 2: "Bird" By Pablo Neruda

It was passed from one bird to another,
the whole gift of the day.
The day went from flute to flute,
went dressed in vegetation,
in flights which opened a tunnel
through the wind would pass
to where birds were breaking open
the dense blue air -
and there, night came in.

When I returned from so many journeys,
I stayed suspended and green
between sun and geography -
I saw how wings worked,
how perfumes are transmitted
by feathery telegraph,
and from above I saw the path,
the springs and the roof tiles,
the fishermen at their trades,
the trousers of the foam;
I saw it all from my green sky.
I had no more alphabet
than the swallows in their courses,
the tiny, shining water
of the small bird on fire
which dances out of the pollen.

Theme: The poem celebrates the vibrant grace of a bird navigating the sky, symbolizing the pure freedom inherent in nature. It highlights how the seamless passage of time unites the living world. Additionally, the poem reveals that us that human language fails to capture the absolute and unspoken wonders of nature.

## 3: "Those Winter Sundays" By Robert Hayden

Sundays too my father got up early
And put his clothes on in the blueblack cold,
then with cracked hands that ached
from labor in the weekday weather made
banked fires blaze. No one ever thanked him.

I'd wake and hear the cold splintering, breaking.
When the rooms were warm, he'd call,
and slowly I would rise and dress,
fearing the chronic angers of that house,

Speaking indifferently to him,
who had driven out the cold
and polished my good shoes as well.
What did I know, what did I know
of love's austere and lonely offices?

The poem depicts quiet parental sacrifice, familial duty and late emotional realization. It portrays a father who endures bitter cold and hard labour every day out of unspoken love for his family. The text also emphasizes how children often fail to understand or appreciate the simple and unglamorous nature of parents devotion or love until they grow up.

## 4: "Landscape with the Fall of Icarus" By William Carlos Williams

According to Brueghel
when Icarus fell
it was spring

a farmer was ploughing
his field
the whole pageantry

of the year was
awake tingling
near

the edge of the sea
concerned
with itself

sweating in the sun
that melted
the wings' wax

unsignificantly
off the coast
there was

a splash quite unnoticed
this was
Icarus drowning

Theme: The central theme of the poem revolves around the profound indifference of the world toward individual human suffering and tragedy. While a massive life-ending tragedy takes place for one person, the rest of the world/society actually remains deeply absorbed in their daily activities. Life relentlessly moves forward as normal and it barely cares about individual tragedy. The poem also highlights that personal catastrophe is ultimately insignificant within the endless cycle of nature and daily human existence.

## 5: "The School Boy" By William Black

I love to rise in a summer morn,
When the birds sing on every tree;
The distant huntsman winds his horn,
And the sky-lark sings with me.
O! what sweet company.

But to go to school in a summer morn,
O! it drives all joy away;
Under a cruel eye outworn.
The little ones spend the day,
In sighing and dismay.

Ah! then at times I drooping sit,
And spend many an anxious hour,
Nor in my book can I take delight,
Nor sit in learnings bower,
Worn thro' with the dreary shower.

How can the bird that is born for joy,
Sit in a cage and sing.
How can a child when fears annoy.
But droop his tender wing.
And forget his youthful spring.

O! father & mother. if buds are nip'd,
And blossoms blown away,
And if the tender plants are strip'd
Of their joy in the springing day,
By sorrow and care's dismay.

How shall the summer arise in joy.
Or the summer fruits appear.
Or how shall we gather what griefs destroy
Or bless the mellowing year.
When the blasts of winter appear.

Theme: The poem highlights the contrast between the freedom of nature and the restrictive environment of a traditional school. It focuses on the importance of childhood innocence and natural learning. Forcing children into monotonous, joyless classrooms destroy their creativity and curiosity. Instead of growing like a healthy plant, a restricted child becomes anxious and fearful. Ultimately, the poem suggests that true education should nurture a child spirit rather than suffocate it with strict rules.

## 6: "Dreams" By Langston Hughes

Hold fast to dreams
For if dreams die
Life is a broken-winged bird
That cannot fly.
Hold fast to dreams
For when dreams go
Life is a barren field
Frozen with snow.

Theme: This poem emphasizes the vital importance of holding onto dreams and aspirations in life. The poet argues that dreams give life its purpose, direction and hope. Without dreams human life becomes static, broken and unfulfilled much like a bird with broken wings that can no longer fly. The poet also compares a dreamless life to a barren and frozen field where nothing can grow fruitfully/successfully. Therefore, the core message of the poem is that people must protect their dreams to survive and thrive.

## 7: "Dreams" DH. Lawrence 

All people dream, but not equally.
Those who dream by night in the dusty recesses of their mind,
Wake in the morning to find that it was vanity.
But the dreamers of the day are dangerous people,
For they dream their dreams with open eyes,
And make them come true.

Theme: The poem depicts two different types of dreamers and the impact of their visions. It compares night dreamers who dream passively at night with day dreamers who dream actively at daylight. The poet illustrates that night dreams are usually subjective, temporary and vanish as soon as the person wakes up to reality. On the other hand, the poet says that day dreamers are dangerous people because they act upon their thoughts with open eyes. The poem ultimately suggests that success belongs to those who actively work hard to turn their conscious dreams into reality.

## 8: "I Wondered Lonely as a Cloud/Daffodils" By William Wordsworth

I wandered lonely as a cloud
That floats on high o'er vales and hills,
When all at once I saw a crowd,
A host, of golden daffodils;
Beside the lake, beneath the trees,
Fluttering and dancing in the breeze.

Continuous as the stars that shine
And twinkle on the milky way,
They stretched in never-ending line
Along the margin of a bay:
Ten thousand saw I at a glance,
Tossing their heads in sprightly dance.

The waves beside them danced; but they
Out-did the sparkling waves in glee:
A poet could not but be gay,
In such a jocund company:
I gazed—and gazed—but little thought
What wealth the show to me had brought:

For oft, when on my couch I lie
In vacant or in pensive mood,
They flash upon that inward eye
Which is the bliss of solitude;
And then my heart with pleasure fills,
And dances with the daffodils.

This romantic poem celebrates the healthy and restorative power of nature on the human mind. The poet describes how a sudden encounter with a field of golden daffodils instantly cures his sense of loneliness. Even long after the experience, the memory of the dancing flowers brings peace and happiness to the poet during his lonely moments. It proves that the bliss of nature is a permanent remedy for human sorrow. 

## 9: "The Lake Isle of Innisfree" By W.B. Yeats

I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree,
And a small cabin build there, of clay and wattles made;
Nine bean-rows will I have there, a hive for the honey-bee,
And live alone in the bee-loud glade.

And I shall have some peace there, for peace comes dropping slow,
Dropping from the veils of the morning to where the cricket sings;
There midnight’s all a glimmer, and noon a purple glow,
And evening full of the linnet’s wings.

I will arise and go now, for always night and day
I hear lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore;
While I stand on the roadway, or on the pavements grey,
I hear it in the deep heart’s core.

Theme: The theme of the poem centers on the deep human longing for peace, tranquility and a return to nature. Living in a chaotic and noisy city, the poet feels spiritually exhausted and suffocated. He wishes to escape to the isolated island of Innisfree, where he can live a simple life close to nature. The poem highlights the conflict between the artificial urban world and the spiritual comfort found in natural surroundings. It shows that the desire for the natural peace lives permanently deep within the human heart. 

## 10: "I Have Seen Bengal's Face" By Jibonanando Das

I have seen Bengal’s face, that is why I do not seek
Beauty of the earth any more: I wake up in the dark
And see the dawn’s magpie-robin perched under the parasol-like huge leaf
Of the fig tree – on all sides I see mounds of leaves of
Black plum – banyan – jackfruit – oak – pipal lying still;
Their shadows fall on the spurge bushes on zedoary clumps;
Who knows when Chand near Champa from his madhukar boat
Saw such oaks – banyans – gamboge’s blue shades
Bengal’s beauty incomparable.

Behula too someday floating on raft on Gangur’s water –
When the fullmoon of the tenebrous twelfth night died on the river’s shoal –
Saw countless pipals and banyans beside the golden corn,
Alas, heard the tender songs of shama – and one day going to Amara.
When she danced like a torn wagtail in Indra’s court
Bengal’s river field, wild violets wept at her feet like anklet bells.

Theme: The poem is about the beautiful nature of Bengal and the poets deep love/affection for his motherland. The poet expresses that as he has seen the the face of Bengal, so he does not wish to see the rest of the world. He beautifully describes local trees, birds and rivers to show that Bengal is like a paradise. The poem also mixes natural beauty with old bengali myths and stories with a simile. Ultimately, the poem highlights the eternal and soul satisfying bond between human beings and their motherland.

## 11: "The Charge of the Light Brigade" By Alfred

I
Half a league, half a league,
Half a league onward,
All in the valley of Death
   Rode the six hundred.
“Forward, the Light Brigade!
Charge for the guns!” he said.
Into the valley of Death
   Rode the six hundred.

II
“Forward, the Light Brigade!”
Was there a man dismayed?
Not though the soldier knew
   Someone had blundered.
   Theirs not to make reply,
   Theirs not to reason why,
   Theirs but to do and die.
   Into the valley of Death
   Rode the six hundred.
(Precised)

Theme: The poem highlights the concept of absolute duty, heroism and patriotic sacrifice in the face of a certain death. True soldiers obey orders with discipline and courage regardless of the technical blunders made by their commanders. It emphasizes that rendering supreme sacrifice for ones nation earns immortal glory and respect from society.

## 12: "How do I Love Thee" By Elizabeth Barrett Browning

How do I love thee? Let me count the ways.
I love thee to the depth and breadth and height
My soul can reach, when feeling out of sight
For the ends of being and ideal grace.
I love thee to the level of every day’s
Most quiet need, by sun and candle-light.
I love thee freely, as men strive for right;
I love thee purely, as they turn from praise.
I love thee with the passion put to use
In my old griefs, and with my childhood’s faith.
I love thee with a love I seemed to lose
With my lost saints. I love thee with the breath,
Smiles, tears, of all my life; and, if God choose,
I shall but love thee better after death.

Theme: The poem focuses on the boundless and unconditional nature of true romantic love. This love exceeds/transcends physical limits, touching every aspect of the soul's daily existence and spiritual aspiration. It suggests that true love stays pure through every storm of life, untouched by sorrow and even after death its presence never truly fades/even after death it continues to exist in quiet, beautiful ways.
